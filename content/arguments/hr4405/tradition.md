<p><strong>H.R.4405 · the Epstein Files Transparency Act</strong></p><p><span data-release-part="119_H_R_4405:description">This bill gives the Attorney General, who leads the Justice Department, 30 days to publish unclassified records about Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell held by the Justice Department, including the FBI, in a form people can search and download. Epstein was a convicted sex offender who died in federal custody in 2019. Maxwell is his associate, who was sentenced to prison. “Unclassified” means not officially kept secret for national defense or foreign policy. The records include flight and travel records, people and organizations connected to Epstein’s crimes or money, deals that ended cases, internal messages about decisions to charge, and records of his detention and death. Records cannot be held back because of embarrassment, harm to reputation or political sensitivity. The bill lists the cases in which parts of records can be held back or blacked out: victims’ identifying information and personal or medical files; child sexual abuse material; material that would harm an active federal investigation or ongoing prosecution, if narrow and temporary; images of death, physical abuse or injury; and properly classified information. A blacked-out part is called a redaction. Each redaction needs a written reason that is published and given to Congress. Within 15 days after the release, the Attorney General must also report to Congress. The report must include a list of government officials and politically exposed persons (people with prominent public roles) named in the released records. Before the bill, the general public-records law, the Freedom of Information Act, let the department keep some law-enforcement information private. In July 2025, the department had said that no further release was appropriate.</span></p><p><strong>Comparison:</strong> <span data-release-part="119_H_R_4405:comparison">If this bill took effect on November 18, 2025, versus law in force then</span></p><details><summary>Bill version and evidence cutoff</summary><p>Engrossed in House · 2025-11-18. Evidence cutoff: 2026-07-17.</p><p><span data-release-part="119_H_R_4405:laterEvidence">Some sources are dated after the comparison date. They are used when they bear on what the bill would have done compared with the law on that date. They do not change the law used for the comparison.</span></p><a href="https://niccdias.github.io/policy-briefs/arguments/hr4405/universalism-nature">Bill and sources</a></details>
<h2>Tradition</h2><ul data-release-part="HR4405_TR:value"><li>to maintain traditional values and ways of thinking</li><li>to follow their family's customs or the customs of a religion</li><li>to honor the traditional practices of their culture</li></ul><p><span data-release-part="HR4405_TR:reference">Read this value as held by a Christian traditionalist in the United States, with the religious right as the cultural reference. Tradition here concerns preserving, observing or passing down identifiable inherited customs, religious practices and ways of life, valued for their continuity with the past. Religious endorsement of a general moral goal alone does not establish that connection.</span></p>
