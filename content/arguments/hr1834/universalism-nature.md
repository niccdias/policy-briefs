title: "H.R.1834 × Universalism – Nature"
---

**Bill:** [H.R.1834](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1834), the House bill extending the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits (introduced as the Breaking the Gridlock Act). Judged as of January 8, 2026, when Congress last voted on it.

**Value:** Universalism – Nature. A person who holds this value finds these things very important:

- to care for nature
- to take part in activities to defend nature
- to protect the natural environment from destruction or pollution

**Survey question:** “Imagine a person named Morgan. It's very important to Morgan [statement]. Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor or oppose H.R.1834?” Each respondent saw one of the statements above.

Eckelman, Kaixin Huang, Robert Lagasse, Emily Senay, Robert Dubrow, and Jodi D. Sherman | Health Affairs, Vol. 39, No. 12, “Health Care Pollution And Public Health Damage In The United States: An Update”, 2020-12-07">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill's added care is small (about 0.05% of U.S. greenhouse gases, by the PRO's own spending-based math), partly plan-switching, and tilted away from hospitals. The sector's total footprint cannot show that the bill adds climate harm to wildlife.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 2:</strong> More prescription use sends more drug residue into waterways, putting fish and water plants at risk.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Insurance increases prescription use. A randomized Medicaid study in Oregon found that gaining coverage led to “an 8.8 percentage point (15%) increase in the probability of taking any prescription drugs” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Taking medicine creates a route into nature. The U.S. Geological Survey explains that drugs enter waterways “primarily through treated municipal wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluent following human excretion or disposal of unwanted pharmaceuticals into the sewer system” <a href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/environmental-health-program/science/complex-mixtures-complex-responses-using" title="U.S. Geological Survey | Environmental Health Program, “Complex Mixtures, Complex Responses—Using Comprehensive Approaches to Assess Pharmaceutical Effects on Fish”, 2015-11-13">[24]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Drug residues can damage water life. EPA reports that hormone-disrupting chemicals can “cause male fish to develop female characteristics” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/household-medication-disposal/impact-pharmaceuticals-released-environment" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (Office of Land and Emergency Management), “The Impact of Pharmaceuticals Released to the Environment”, 2023-06-12">[25]</a>. Antibiotics “are likewise poisonous to aquatic plants” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/household-medication-disposal/impact-pharmaceuticals-released-environment" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (Office of Land and Emergency Management), “The Impact of Pharmaceuticals Released to the Environment”, 2023-06-12">[25]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> By enabling more prescription use, the bill increases the flow of drug residues into waterways and the risks to the fish and plants Morgan wants to defend.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The PRO's drug evidence comes from a different kind of coverage and from factory-fed sewage, not from what this bill changes.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The Oregon study gave sick people free care: “OHP Standard provides relatively comprehensive benefits with no consumer cost sharing.” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a> And “Most strikingly, they appear to be in quite poor health” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill draws in healthier people. With the extension, CBO expects “the people who enroll in the marketplaces would be healthier” <a href="https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61999" title="Congressional Budget Office, cost estimate, January 8, 2026">[3]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Many of the bill's enrollees pay large bills before their plan pays. People earning over 400% of the poverty line, whom the bill makes eligible again, “face average deductibles of about $5,300 for benchmark coverage” <a href="https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/2025-12/Enhanced%20Premium%20Tax%20Credits%20Direct%20Almost%20All%20of%20Their%20Benefit%20to%20Lower-%20and%20Moderate-Income%20Americans_1218.pdf" title="urban.org, analysis, December 2025">[26]</a>. A deductible is what a person pays before insurance starts paying.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's field evidence of harm to fish came from a sewage plant fed by a drug factory. The fish were exposed to “treated municipal effluent from a WWTP known to receive input from a pharmaceutical formulation facility” <a href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/environmental-health-program/science/complex-mixtures-complex-responses-using" title="U.S. Geological Survey | Environmental Health Program, “Complex Mixtures, Complex Responses—Using Comprehensive Approaches to Assess Pharmaceutical Effects on Fish”, 2015-11-13">[24]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The same source ties the highest drug levels to factory waste: drugs “can be locally elevated in the effluent at WWTPs that receive incoming waste from pharmaceutical formulation facilities” <a href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/environmental-health-program/science/complex-mixtures-complex-responses-using" title="U.S. Geological Survey | Environmental Health Program, “Complex Mixtures, Complex Responses—Using Comprehensive Approaches to Assess Pharmaceutical Effects on Fish”, 2015-11-13">[24]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Pouring drugs down the drain, another route the PRO names, is already banned at care sites for hazardous drugs: “EPA prohibited all healthcare facilities and reverse distributors from disposing of their hazardous waste pharmaceuticals down the drain” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-05/GA%20Sewer%20Ban%20Fact%20Sheet_final.pdf" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “EPA&#x27;s Ban on Sewering Pharmaceuticals: Introductory Factsheet (EPA 530-F-22-003)”, 2022-04">[27]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Oregon's jump in drug use overstates what this bill would cause. The PRO has not shown that the bill raises drug levels in streams enough to harm fish or water plants.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 3:</strong> More hospital care creates more waste that can pollute the environment.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Added insurance can increase hospital use. The randomized Oregon study found that gaining coverage led to “a 2.1 percentage point (30%) increase in the probability of having a hospital admission” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Hospital care uses many throwaway supplies: “From plastic speculums to gloves and gowns, hospitals use thousands of disposable items each day.” <a href="https://washingtondc.jhu.edu/news/how-the-health-care-system-can-help-reduce-climate-change/" title="Johns Hopkins University Bloomberg Center (Johns Hopkins in Washington, D.C.), “How the health care system can help reduce climate change”, 2024-04-23">[28]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Some medical waste is hazardous. WHO reports: “High-income countries generate on average up to 0.5 kg of hazardous waste per hospital bed per day” <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-care-waste" title="World Health Organization, “Health-care waste”, 2024-10-24">[29]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Waste disposal creates pollution risks: “Treatment and disposal of health-care waste may pose health risks indirectly through the release of pathogens and toxic pollutants into the environment.” <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-care-waste" title="World Health Organization, “Health-care waste”, 2024-10-24">[29]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> The extra hospital care creates more waste to handle and dispose of. That adds to the risk of releasing pollution into the natural environment Morgan wants to protect.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> More coverage does not reliably mean more hospital stays, and hospital waste harms nature mainly when handled badly, which U.S. law limits.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> When the ACA expanded Medicaid, total hospital stays did not rise: “Although we found no change in total discharges, we did find a reduction in diabetes-related discharges.” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0183616" title="Seth Freedman, Sayeh Nikpay, Aaron Carroll, and Kosali Simon | PLOS ONE, “Changes in inpatient payer-mix and hospitalizations following Medicaid expansion: Evidence from all-capture hospital discharge data”, 2017-09-28">[30]</a> A discharge is a completed hospital stay.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The Oregon authors warn that their findings may not hold for large programs: “the effects of much larger health insurance expansions might differ” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Most hospital waste is ordinary trash: “about 85% is general, non-hazardous waste comparable to domestic waste” <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-care-waste" title="World Health Organization, “Health-care waste”, 2024-10-24">[29]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> WHO ties waste pollution to poor handling: “Open burning and low-temperature incineration of health care wastes can, under some circumstances, result in the emission of dioxins, furans and particulate matter.” <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-care-waste" title="World Health Organization, “Health-care waste”, 2024-10-24">[29]</a> Landfills taint water “if those landfills are not properly constructed” <a href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-care-waste" title="World Health Organization, “Health-care waste”, 2024-10-24">[29]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> U.S. law has shut most medical waste incinerators. EPA counted “approximately 2,400 HMIWI operating in the United States” in 1997 but only “57 currently operating HMIWI” in 2009 <a href="https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-12/documents/hmiwi_fact_sheet_091509.pdf" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Office of Air Quality Planning and Standards, “Fact Sheet: Final Amendments to New Source Performance Standards and Emission Guidelines for Hospital, Medical, and Infectious Waste Incinerators”, 2009-09-15">[31]</a>. HMIWI are hospital and medical waste incinerators.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Those left must “control emissions of hydrogen chloride, carbon monoxide, lead, cadmium, mercury, particulate matter, dioxins/furans, nitrogen oxides, and sulfur dioxide” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-12/documents/hmiwi_fact_sheet_091509.pdf" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Office of Air Quality Planning and Standards, “Fact Sheet: Final Amendments to New Source Performance Standards and Emission Guidelines for Hospital, Medical, and Infectious Waste Incinerators”, 2009-09-15">[31]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Neither more hospital stays nor more harmful waste releases follow from this bill. The bill changes no waste rule.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 4:</strong> More care also adds pollution that makes soil and water more acidic.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Added coverage raises use of medical services. The Oregon study found that gaining coverage led to “a 21 percentage point (35%) increase in the probability of having an outpatient visit” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535298/" title="Amy Finkelstein and colleagues | The Quarterly Journal of Economics; full text via PubMed Central, “The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: Evidence from the First Year”, 2012-05-03">[22]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Supplying care requires energy and manufacturing: “the health care system also uses vast quantities of energy-intensive goods and services, such as pharmaceuticals and medical devices, which require significant energy inputs for their manufacturing.” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> This produces acid-forming pollution. A 2016 U.S. study estimated health care's share of national pollution impacts in 2013 included “acid rain (12%)” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Acid rain changes soil and water. The study tracks “environmental effects of acidification (from formation and deposition of acid rain) and eutrophication (algae blooms from excess nutrients) in soils and surface waters” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill supports more care and the power and manufacturing it requires. That adds acid-forming pollution to the soil and water Morgan wants to keep clean.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The acid-rain share is a 2013 estimate built on 2002 pollution rates, so it does not show that the bill's added visits harm soil or water now.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The study did not measure pollution from care. It assumed it from spending: “emissions are directly proportional to spending in EIOLCA models and are based on a single year (2002 in this case)” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a>. EIOLCA is the study's model for turning spending into pollution.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Power plants drive health care's acid pollution. Power generation “is the largest contributing supply sector by far to acidification (44%)” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Power plants have since slashed sulfur dioxide, which “contributes to the formation of acid rain” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/power-sector/progress-report-emissions-reductions" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Office of Air and Radiation, “Power Sector Programs Progress Report - Emissions Reductions”, 2024-09-12 (last updated; data current through 2023)">[32]</a>. By 2023 they had cut it by “10.6 million tons (94 percent) from 2000 levels” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/power-sector/progress-report-emissions-reductions" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Office of Air and Radiation, “Power Sector Programs Progress Report - Emissions Reductions”, 2024-09-12 (last updated; data current through 2023)">[32]</a>, while “electricity generation from these power plants remained relatively stable from 2000 through 2023” <a href="https://www.epa.gov/power-sector/progress-report-emissions-reductions" title="U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Office of Air and Radiation, “Power Sector Programs Progress Report - Emissions Reductions”, 2024-09-12 (last updated; data current through 2023)">[32]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The authors admit other industries cleaned up too: “many other sectors have also experienced changes in emissions factors due to fuel switching, efficiency improvements, and improved pollution controls” <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157014" title="Matthew J. Eckelman and Jodi Sherman | PLOS ONE, “Environmental Impacts of the U.S. Health Care System and Effects on Public Health”, 2016-06-09">[21]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Each dollar of care now causes far less acid pollution than the PRO's figure assumes. The bill changes none of the limits behind that drop.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td></td><td><p><strong>New point:</strong> The PRO's own pollution experts say to cut health care pollution without cutting access to care.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill only extends premium help: “EXTENSION OF ENHANCED PREMIUM TAX CREDIT” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr1834/BILLS-119hr1834eh.htm" title="H.R.1834, bill text, January 8, 2026">[1]</a>. It changes no rule on power plants, waste, or drugs.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's climate source names the fixes: “the health care sector should decrease unnecessary consumption of resources, decarbonize power generation, and invest in preventive care” <a href="https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2020.01247" title="Matthew J. Eckelman, Kaixin Huang, Robert Lagasse, Emily Senay, Robert Dubrow, and Jodi D. Sherman | Health Affairs, Vol. 39, No. 12, “Health Care Pollution And Public Health Damage In The United States: An Update”, 2020-12-07">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The same authors reject cutting access to lower pollution: “reducing health care pollution must never be achieved at the expense of equitable access to high-quality care” <a href="https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2020.01247" title="Matthew J. Eckelman, Kaixin Huang, Robert Lagasse, Emily Senay, Robert Dubrow, and Jodi D. Sherman | Health Affairs, Vol. 39, No. 12, “Health Care Pollution And Public Health Damage In The United States: An Update”, 2020-12-07">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Caring for nature points Morgan toward cleaner power and less waste in health care, not toward opposing a bill that only helps people pay premiums.</li></ul></td></tr>
