title: "S.2 × Security – Personal"
---

**Bill:** [S.2](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2), the Secure America Act. Judged as of June 9, 2026, when Congress last voted on it.

**Survey question:** Each respondent saw one of these three versions, with a randomly assigned name in place of [NAME].

1. Imagine a person named [NAME]. It's very important to [NAME] to avoid disease and protect their health. Does this value give [NAME] a reason to favor or oppose S.2?
1. Imagine a person named [NAME]. It's very important to [NAME] to be personally safe and secure. Does this value give [NAME] a reason to favor or oppose S.2?
1. Imagine a person named [NAME]. It's very important to [NAME] to avoid anything dangerous. Does this value give [NAME] a reason to favor or oppose S.2?

## Does this value give [NAME] a reason to favor S.2?

<table class="pc">
<tr><th>PRO</th><th>CON</th></tr>
<tr class="main"><td><p><span class="lbl">Main effect</span> S.2 adds money for existing border protection, criminal investigations, and immigration enforcement through September 2029. Its appropriations are “In addition to amounts otherwise available” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a> and “to remain available until September 30, 2029” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>. Section 102’s investigation funds cover work outside immigration and customs enforcement: “functions other than those related to Homeland Security Investigations' immigration enforcement and customs enforcement missions.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a></p></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Main effect</span> Not disputed.</p></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 1</span> Added drug scanners strengthen protection against fentanyl poisoning.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 103 funds new scanners and drug interception: “Procurement and integration of new nonintrusive inspection equipment” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a> and “combating drug trafficking, including fentanyl and its precursor chemicals” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Scanners have helped uncover hidden fentanyl. In a 2024 vehicle inspection, “CBP officers used non-intrusive technology to screen the sedan revealing anomalies inside all four tires of the vehicle.” <a href="https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/port-san-luis-cbp-officers-bust-fentanyl-and-meth-smuggling-attempts" title="U.S. Customs and Border Protection, “Port of San Luis CBP Officers Bust Fentanyl and Meth Smuggling Attempts”, 2024-09-05">[2]</a> Officers recovered packages from the tires: “Four of the packages contained nearly 54,000 blue fentanyl pills” <a href="https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/port-san-luis-cbp-officers-bust-fentanyl-and-meth-smuggling-attempts" title="U.S. Customs and Border Protection, “Port of San Luis CBP Officers Bust Fentanyl and Meth Smuggling Attempts”, 2024-09-05">[2]</a>.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> These drugs pose a hidden, lethal health risk: “Drugs may contain deadly levels of fentanyl, and you wouldn't be able to see it, taste it, or smell it.” <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/stop-overdose/caring/fentanyl-facts.html" title="Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, “Fentanyl Facts”, 2024-04-02 (date displayed on page; fetch metadata also records 2024-06-20)">[3]</a></li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Finding and seizing concealed drug loads keeps those drugs out of circulation. Funding more of this work strengthens protection against a poison in the U.S. drug supply, serving [NAME]’s wish to protect their health.</li></ul></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Response</span> A successful seizure does not establish the drop in fentanyl exposure that the PRO attributes to more scanners.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 103 funds “Procurement and integration of new nonintrusive inspection equipment” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>. Equipment purchases and usable scanning capacity are different: GAO’s January 2026 review reports, “Some factors have limited the use of the systems, such as limited staff and system outages.” <a href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108767/index.html" title="Hilary M. Benedict / U.S. Government Accountability Office, “Border Security: Improvements Needed to Increase Vehicle Scanning at Land Ports of Entry (GAO-26-108767)”, 2026-01-22">[4]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Seized fentanyl can be replaced. Jonathan Caulkins explains that synthetic opioids “permit seizures to be replaced in days” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/FP-20241127-supply-control-caulkins.pdf" title="Jonathan P. Caulkins / Brookings Institution, “How should drug policy respond to surging supplies of dangerous drugs?”, 2024-12-04 (publication date shown on the accompanying report page)">[5]</a>. The number seized does not show how much less fentanyl buyers can obtain.</li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> The PRO leaves two links unsupported: how this funding adds usable scanning beyond existing resources, and how added seizures reduce consumers’ exposure after suppliers respond. Its seized-pill example does not establish the added health protection.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 2</span> Added funding for criminal investigations strengthens protection against poisoned pills sold as real medicine.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 102 funds Homeland Security Investigations (HSI), ICE’s criminal investigation branch: “to hire, pay, train, and equip Homeland Security Investigations agents and support personnel” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> HSI and its partners have investigated fake pharmacies selling pills made in U.S. drug factories. Their report describes the seizure of pills from those factories: “Law enforcement raided at least three of these pill mills and two other narcotics storage locations, seizing approximately 625,000 counterfeit pills” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-new-york-federal-partners-announce-charges-against-18-defendants-scheme" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “HSI New York, Federal Partners Announce Charges Against 18 Defendants in Scheme to Manufacture, Distribute Millions of Deadly Counterfeit Pharmaceuticals Through Fake Online Pharmacies”, 2024-10-01 (page updated 2025-02-27)">[6]</a>.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> This fraud reaches people seeking health care. The report describes victims “who were in need of medication — people suffering with legitimate ailments.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-new-york-federal-partners-announce-charges-against-18-defendants-scheme" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “HSI New York, Federal Partners Announce Charges Against 18 Defendants in Scheme to Manufacture, Distribute Millions of Deadly Counterfeit Pharmaceuticals Through Fake Online Pharmacies”, 2024-10-01 (page updated 2025-02-27)">[6]</a> It reports, “At least nine of the victims later died of narcotics poisoning.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-new-york-federal-partners-announce-charges-against-18-defendants-scheme" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “HSI New York, Federal Partners Announce Charges Against 18 Defendants in Scheme to Manufacture, Distribute Millions of Deadly Counterfeit Pharmaceuticals Through Fake Online Pharmacies”, 2024-10-01 (page updated 2025-02-27)">[6]</a></li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Added funding supports finding these sellers and removing their poisoned pills from sale. That protects people seeking medicine, giving [NAME] a health reason to favor this added capacity.</li></ul></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Response</span> The PRO does not connect its pharmacy case to work that Section 102’s restricted funds may pay for.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> The money must support “functions other than those related to Homeland Security Investigations' immigration enforcement and customs enforcement missions.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a> Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) is the criminal investigation branch cited by the PRO; not every HSI activity is an allowed use of this money.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> The case concerns an international drug operation: “Lopez Reyes led the enterprise, orchestrating and controlling every facet of the scheme from the Dominican Republic.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-new-york-federal-partners-announce-charges-against-18-defendants-scheme" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “HSI New York, Federal Partners Announce Charges Against 18 Defendants in Scheme to Manufacture, Distribute Millions of Deadly Counterfeit Pharmaceuticals Through Fake Online Pharmacies”, 2024-10-01 (page updated 2025-02-27)">[6]</a> The report also says “the defendants shipped counterfeit pharmaceuticals to victims across the United States and around the world” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-new-york-federal-partners-announce-charges-against-18-defendants-scheme" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “HSI New York, Federal Partners Announce Charges Against 18 Defendants in Scheme to Manufacture, Distribute Millions of Deadly Counterfeit Pharmaceuticals Through Fake Online Pharmacies”, 2024-10-01 (page updated 2025-02-27)">[6]</a>.</li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Locating pill factories in U.S. basements does not place the investigation outside customs work. The PRO identifies no part of this international case that the restricted funds would expand outside the excluded missions.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 3</span> More capacity to hold and remove convicted violent offenders limits their opportunities for further attacks.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 202 funds enforcement staff and “transportation costs and related costs associated with alien departure or removal operations.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> This work includes custody after a criminal sentence ends. ICE reported that it “apprehended an illegally present Dominican national convicted for murder in Massachusetts in 2007 when officers arrested Cesar Augusto Polanco, 59, January 24 at the Massachusetts Correctional Institution in Norfolk after he was released from state custody.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/ice-boston-arrests-illegal-dominican-national-convicted-murder-massachusetts" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “ICE Boston arrests illegal Dominican national convicted of murder in Massachusetts”, 2025-02-03 (page updated 2025-06-13)">[7]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> ICE kept him in custody for the removal process: “Polanco remains in ICE custody pending removal proceedings.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/ice-boston-arrests-illegal-dominican-national-convicted-murder-massachusetts" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “ICE Boston arrests illegal Dominican national convicted of murder in Massachusetts”, 2025-02-03 (page updated 2025-06-13)">[7]</a></li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Holding convicted violent offenders after prison release, and carrying out their removal, separates them from people they could attack here. Added funding for staff and transport backs that protection for U.S. residents such as [NAME].</li></ul></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Response</span> One existing detention does not establish that S.2’s broader removal funding reduces residents’ exposure to violence.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> The February 2025 report says, “Polanco remains in ICE custody pending removal proceedings.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/ice-boston-arrests-illegal-dominican-national-convicted-murder-massachusetts" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “ICE Boston arrests illegal Dominican national convicted of murder in Massachusetts”, 2025-02-03 (page updated 2025-06-13)">[7]</a> That report documents custody achieved before S.2, not a detention that depends on it.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Existing law already supplied detention funding. CRS lists the 2025 law’s “$45 billion for detention capacity” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48874/R48874.3.pdf" title="Congressional Research Service, report R48874, June 24, 2026">[8]</a>. The PRO does not show that S.2’s added staff and transport would prevent a loss of custody or hold a violent offender who would otherwise be released.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Research on a comparable detention-and-deportation program found, “The results show that the Secure Communities program has had no observable effect on the overall crime rate.” <a href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/680935" title="Thomas J. Miles and Adam B. Cox / The Journal of Law and Economics, 57(4), “Does Immigration Enforcement Reduce Crime? Evidence from Secure Communities”, 2014-11 (journal issue; fetched page also displays 2015-10-15)">[9]</a> A separate study of violent victimization in 2005–2014 reports, “The activation of 287(g) jail enforcement agreements and anti-detainer policies had no significant impact on violent victimization risk during the period.” <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11309025/" title="Eric P. Baumer and Min Xie / Criminology &amp; Public Policy, “Federal-Local Partnerships on Immigration Law Enforcement: Are the Policies Effective in Reducing Violent Victimization?”, 2023-03-14 (final edited publication; author manuscript available in PMC 2024-08-08)">[10]</a></li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> The example establishes an existing separation from the public. The broader studies do not supply the missing evidence that this additional removal funding reduces violence; the PRO cannot infer that result from the single detention.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 4</span> Supporting transfers from jails makes safer arrests possible.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 202 funds partnerships with state and local police: “Supporting coordination with state and local authorities by expanding, facilitating, and implementing agreements under section 287(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> One form of these agreements lets trained local officers identify people in jail for immigration proceedings. ICE explains that they “place them into immigration proceedings at the time of release from state or local custody.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/doclib/about/offices/ero/287g/factsheetJEM.pdf" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, Enforcement and Removal Operations, “287(g) Jail Enforcement Model — ERO Facts”, 2025-02 (document: Cleared for external use February 2025)">[11]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> A transfer can avoid a dangerous arrest in public. In the Polanco case, ICE said cooperation let officers “make the arrest in the safety of a jail cell rather than sending a team of officers into the community to make a potentially dangerous arrest.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/ice-boston-arrests-illegal-dominican-national-convicted-murder-massachusetts" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “ICE Boston arrests illegal Dominican national convicted of murder in Massachusetts”, 2025-02-03 (page updated 2025-06-13)">[7]</a></li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Funding these partnerships supports more arrests in controlled settings. Keeping those confrontations out of public places protects nearby residents, giving [NAME] a separate reason grounded in personal safety.</li></ul></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Response</span> Expanding 287(g) agreements does not establish that public arrests would be replaced by jail transfers.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> The cited transfer already occurred through cooperation: “The Massachusetts Correctional Institution honored the immigration detainer and informed ICE Boston of Polanco’s release.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/ice-boston-arrests-illegal-dominican-national-convicted-murder-massachusetts" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “ICE Boston arrests illegal Dominican national convicted of murder in Massachusetts”, 2025-02-03 (page updated 2025-06-13)">[7]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> These agreements give local officers some immigration powers, including outside jails. ICE’s February 2026 description says, “The Task Force Model allows your officers to enforce limited immigration authority while performing routine police duties or as an active participant in an ICE-led task force.” <a href="https://www.ice.gov/doclib/about/offices/ero/287g/participantMap.pdf" title="U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, “287(g) Participating Agencies (as of 5 February 2026)”, 2026-02-05 (date displayed in document)">[12]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 202 funds agreements generally. It also funds “release monitoring, transportation, and arrests of covered unlawful aliens encountered in jurisdictions that are not qualified cooperating jurisdictions” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>. It does not direct officers to replace public arrests with jail transfers.</li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> More authority to make immigration arrests is different from moving the same arrests into jails. The PRO’s example shows the safety of an existing transfer, not a change in arrest setting caused by S.2.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 5</span> Funding trafficking investigations strengthens protection against forced work and sexual violence.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 102 pays for HSI agents and support staff, including “other necessary expenses for Homeland Security Investigations' mission support and operations and maintenance” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>. DHS says HSI “conducts criminal human trafficking investigations worldwide.” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Trafficking is a danger within U.S. life: “many human traffickers and victims are U.S. citizens.” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a> Contact can begin through ordinary relationships or job searches: “They can be relatives, romantic partners, or close family friends. Or they can be the people behind an employment ad or a new friend on social media or online gaming.” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Traffickers’ control includes “actual physical assaults and sexual violence” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a>. Identifying victims also opens a route to help, including “medical and mental health care, safety planning, and legal services” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a>.</li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Added funding supports identifying violent exploitation and bringing victims into protection and care. Protection against abuse through everyday work and relationships serves the personal safety [NAME] values.</li></ul></td><td><p><span class="lbl">Response</span> A duty to assist identified trafficking victims does not establish that S.2 brings more victims into protection and care.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> The DHS source makes assistance conditional on victims being in investigations: “All victims in HSI investigations receive statutorily mandated assistance” <a href="https://www.dhs.gov/human-trafficking-quick-facts" title="U.S. Department of Homeland Security, “Human Trafficking Quick Facts”, 2025-05-22 (publication date from search metadata)">[13]</a>. Its list of referrals describes help already required by law after victims are found.</li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Finding victims and securing their help are separate steps. GAO’s 2016 review reports that “identifying and distinguishing human trafficking from other crimes such as prostitution can be challenging” <a href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-555.pdf" title="U.S. Government Accountability Office, “Human Trafficking: Agencies Have Taken Steps to Assess Prevalence, Address Victim Issues, and Avoid Grant Duplication (GAO-16-555)”, 2016-06 (report cover date)">[14]</a>, and that victims “may be unable or unwilling to testify due to distrust of law enforcement or fear of retaliation by the trafficker.” <a href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-555.pdf" title="U.S. Government Accountability Office, “Human Trafficking: Agencies Have Taken Steps to Assess Prevalence, Address Victim Issues, and Avoid Grant Duplication (GAO-16-555)”, 2016-06 (report cover date)">[14]</a></li><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 102 funds agents and “mission support and operations and maintenance” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>. The PRO identifies no change in how these funds would uncover trafficking or overcome the barriers between victims and help.</li><li class="so"><span class="lbl">So</span> Describing referrals for people already found does not fill the missing link from added HSI payroll to more victims reaching safety. The claimed wider protection through work and relationships remains unsupported.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><span class="lbl">Point 6</span> Stronger gang investigations protect people from violence in their communities.</p><ul><li class="because"><span class="lbl">Because</span> Section 102 funds HSI agents “to hire, pay, train, and equip” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2/BILLS-119s2es.htm" title="S.2, bill text, June 5, 2026">[1]</a>.
