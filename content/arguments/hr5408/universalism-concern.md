title: "H.R.5408 × Universalism – Concern"
---

**Bill:** [H.R.5408](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5408), the Faster Labor Contracts Act. Judged as of June 9, 2026, when Congress last voted on it.

**Value:** Universalism – Concern. A person who holds this value finds these things very important:

- that the weak and vulnerable in society be protected
- that every person in the world have equal opportunities in life
- that everyone be treated justly, even people they don't know

**Survey question:** “Imagine a person named Morgan. It's very important to Morgan [statement]. Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor or oppose H.R.5408?” Each respondent saw one of the statements above.

## Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor H.R.5408?

<table class="pc">
<tr><th>PRO</th><th>CON</th></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Claim:</strong> Morgan's concern for vulnerable people, equal opportunity, and just treatment of strangers gives Morgan a reason to favor H.R.5408.</p></td><td><p><strong>Claim:</strong> The PRO fails to show that H.R.5408 protects vulnerable workers: its binding process has no deadline, relies on a gutted agency, and can lock workers into terms they never approved.</p></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Main effect:</strong> For newly recognized unions covered by the National Labor Relations Act, the bill creates a path to a first union contract through deadlines, mediation (help from a neutral third party), and arbitration (a panel setting binding terms): “This bill establishes mandatory deadlines for parties negotiating an initial collective bargaining agreement (CBA) and provides for mediation and arbitration to finalize CBAs.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5408" title="Congressional Research Service, bill summary, June 9, 2026">[1]</a> The panel's decision binds both sides for two years: “such decision shall be binding upon the parties for a period of 2 years” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</p></td><td><p><strong>Main effect:</strong> Not disputed.</p></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 1:</strong> It protects workers from employers who use delay to deny them a first contract.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Current law requires bargaining, but not agreement. A federal appeals court explains: “employers must bargain in good faith, but they are never required to agree to any particular terms.” <a href="https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/23/23-60132-CV0.pdf" title="United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit; opinion by Andrew S. Oldham, “Thryv, Incorporated v. National Labor Relations Board, No. 23-60132”, 2024-05-24">[3]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The gap is large. An Economic Policy Institute review reports that a study of 2018 union elections found “63% of unions failed to reach a first contract within one year of winning their election.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/union-first-contract-fact-sheet/" title="Celine McNicholas, Margaret Poydock, and John Schmitt | Economic Policy Institute, “Workers are winning union elections, but it can take years to get their first contract”, 2023-05-01">[4]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Delay gives employers power over workers. The bill's findings state: “The employers can use those delays to sap labor organization resolve and secure more favorable terms for the employer.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> Binding settlement removes the employer's ability to defeat a first contract simply by withholding agreement. That protects the weaker side and makes workers' lawful choice count, including for workers Morgan will never meet.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill does not end delay; it adds an arbitration stage with no deadline and puts a clock on honest bargaining too.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill gives the sides 14 days to pick the panel but sets no deadline for its decision. As Rep. Fine said, “there is no time limit on the arbitration, which could last for months or even years.” <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2026-06-09/html/CREC-2026-06-09-pt1-PgH4029.htm" title="Fine, floor speech, Congressional Record, June 9, 2026">[5]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The clock runs in every first-contract talk, not just stalled ones. With no finding of stalling, “either party may notify the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service that a dispute exists, and may request mediation.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's own source admits that not all delay is stalling: “the two sides have different objective interests regarding many of the issues covered, all of which can take time to negotiate.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/union-first-contract-fact-sheet/" title="Celine McNicholas, Margaret Poydock, and John Schmitt | Economic Policy Institute, “Workers are winning union elections, but it can take years to get their first contract”, 2023-05-01">[4]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A short, fixed clock can push workers into worse deals. Heritage testimony warns it “could result in workers and employers either agreeing to or being forced into contracts that are worse than they would have achieved on their own timeline.” <a href="https://www.heritage.org/government-regulation/report/labor-law-reform-part-1-diagnosing-the-issues-exploring-current" title="Heritage Foundation, “Labor Law Reform Part 1: Diagnosing the Issues, Exploring Current Proposals | The Heritage Foundation”, October 9, 2025">[6]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill swaps employer delay for a new wait with no end date, and lets an employer send talks to a panel after about four months. It does not reliably protect the weaker side.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 2:</strong> It protects workers in jobs with frequent staff changes from losing their union win to delay.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Staff turnover can weaken a new union. EPI explains: “High turnover means the employer can recruit new workers who did not participate in the successful organizing campaign.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/union-first-contract-fact-sheet/" title="Celine McNicholas, Margaret Poydock, and John Schmitt | Economic Policy Institute, “Workers are winning union elections, but it can take years to get their first contract”, 2023-05-01">[4]</a> Those workers “may not identify as strongly with the new union, directly reducing the union’s bargaining power.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/union-first-contract-fact-sheet/" title="Celine McNicholas, Margaret Poydock, and John Schmitt | Economic Policy Institute, “Workers are winning union elections, but it can take years to get their first contract”, 2023-05-01">[4]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill keeps the employer's duty to bargain in place unless workers vote to remove their union: “an employer's duty to collectively bargain shall continue absent decertification of the representative following an election conducted pursuant to section 9” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The contract process and continuing duty to bargain protect workers' collective voice from being worn away as staff leave. That gives workers vulnerable to delay a fairer chance to secure the contract they organized for.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill protects a past vote, not the wishes of the workers there now.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Under current law, an employer can stop bargaining only on proof that the union has lost majority support: “an employer may lawfully withdraw recognition only where the union has actually lost the support of a majority of the unit employees.” <a href="https://www.kmm.com/nlrb-adopts-new-standard-under-which-employers-may-unilaterally-withdraw-recognition-from-incumbent-union/" title="Kauff McGuire &amp; Margolis LLP, “NLRB Adopts New Standard Under Which Employers May Unilaterally Withdraw Recognition From Incumbent Union”, 2001-04-05">[7]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill closes that route. The duty to bargain continues “absent decertification of the representative following an election conducted pursuant to section 9” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>. Decertification is a government-run vote to remove the union.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> In high-turnover jobs, many workers arrive after the union vote. The bill “could easily create situations where workers who never voted for a union get stuck with one anyway.” <a href="https://cei.org/blog/the-faster-labor-contracts-act-would-force-workers-into-unions-they-never-voted-for/" title="Competitive Enterprise Institute, analysis, June 3, 2026">[8]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> Even after most current workers have turned against the union, they stay bound until a new government election, and can be bound to a contract set by a panel that union helped pick. That is not fair treatment of the workers there now.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 3:</strong> It requires those setting contract terms to consider workers' ability to support their families.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Both sides help choose the panel: “one member selected by the individual or labor organization, one member selected by the employer, and one neutral member mutually agreed to by the parties” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Its decision must weigh “the employees' cost of living” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a> and “the employees' ability to sustain themselves, their families, and their dependents on the wages and benefits they earn from the employer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The decision must also weigh the business's means and pay at similar firms: “the employer's financial status and prospects” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a> and “the wages and benefits other employers in the same business provide their employees” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Workers' basic needs become a required part of the decision. A process that hears both sides and weighs those needs serves Morgan's concern for vulnerable families and fair treatment.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> Weighing family needs guarantees workers nothing, and workers get no vote on the result.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Family needs are one of five factors, with no more weight than “the employer's financial status and prospects” or “the wages and benefits other employers in the same business provide their employees.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a> The bill sets no pay floor.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The union's pick is one vote of three, so the neutral member tips the balance: “the arbitrator has the most power because he or she will choose contract terms that align more closely either with the employer or with the union.” <a href="https://www.heritage.org/government-regulation/report/labor-law-reform-part-1-diagnosing-the-issues-exploring-current" title="Heritage Foundation, “Labor Law Reform Part 1: Diagnosing the Issues, Exploring Current Proposals | The Heritage Foundation”, October 9, 2025">[6]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Workers cannot vote the result down. A panel majority decides, and “such decision shall be binding upon the parties for a period of 2 years, unless amended during such period by written consent of the parties.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> That takes away a vote workers have now: “nearly all union constitutions and bylaws require union members’ ratification of collective bargaining agreements before they are signed and enforced.” <a href="https://www.heritage.org/government-regulation/report/labor-law-reform-part-1-diagnosing-the-issues-exploring-current" title="Heritage Foundation, “Labor Law Reform Part 1: Diagnosing the Issues, Exploring Current Proposals | The Heritage Foundation”, October 9, 2025">[6]</a> Ratification is a member vote to approve the contract.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The imposed terms last beyond two years. The panel's contract “will become the baseline for all future contracts.” <a href="https://www.laborrelationsupdate.com/2026/06/faster-is-not-always-better-house-passes-bill-seeking-radical-change-in-first-contract-bargaining/" title="Michael Lebowich, Joshua Fox, Daniel H. Dorson, and Dixie Morrison | Proskauer, Labor Relations Update, “Faster is Not Always Better: House Passes Bill Seeking Radical Change in First Contract Bargaining”, 2026-06-11">[9]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> Workers can be bound to pay too low for their families, with no vote and no way out for two years without the employer's consent. Those terms then become the starting point for later talks.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 4:</strong> It protects workers' existing pay and hours while they seek a contract.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Current law lets employers put their last offer into effect after a genuine deadlock in talks. The appeals court describes “unilateral changes by imposing their last best, final offer” <a href="https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/23/23-60132-CV0.pdf" title="United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit; opinion by Andrew S. Oldham, “Thryv, Incorporated v. National Labor Relations Board, No. 23-60132”, 2024-05-24">[3]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill adds a duty “to maintain current wages, hours, and terms and conditions of employment pending an agreement” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Workers gain protection from cuts during talks. That matters to Morgan because workers who cannot afford a pay cut gain security while seeking fair terms.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The pay rule is a pay freeze that current law already applies during talks; the bill only makes it last longer.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> During first-contract talks, current law already bars cuts: employers “are expected not to take away or to grant wage increases and benefits improvements” <a href="https://www.bsk.com/uploads/Law360---Conflicting-NLRB-Stances-Create-Employer-Compliance-Plight.pdf" title="Alice Stock, of counsel, Bond Schoeneck &amp; King PLLC (former NLRB deputy general counsel) | Law360 (reprinted by Bond Schoeneck &amp; King), “Conflicting NLRB Stances Create Employer Compliance Plight”, 2023-07-25">[10]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The same rule blocks raises. Starbucks raised pay for non-union staff but said it “could not give those same improvements to its unionizing or union-represented employees because of its legal obligations to maintain the status quo” <a href="https://www.bsk.com/uploads/Law360---Conflicting-NLRB-Stances-Create-Employer-Compliance-Plight.pdf" title="Alice Stock, of counsel, Bond Schoeneck &amp; King PLLC (former NLRB deputy general counsel) | Law360 (reprinted by Bond Schoeneck &amp; King), “Conflicting NLRB Stances Create Employer Compliance Plight”, 2023-07-25">[10]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Today the freeze can end after a genuine deadlock in talks (called an impasse), when raises may be “implemented after an overall good faith impasse in bargaining a collective bargaining agreement” <a href="https://www.bsk.com/uploads/Law360---Conflicting-NLRB-Stances-Create-Employer-Compliance-Plight.pdf" title="Alice Stock, of counsel, Bond Schoeneck &amp; King PLLC (former NLRB deputy general counsel) | Law360 (reprinted by Bond Schoeneck &amp; King), “Conflicting NLRB Stances Create Employer Compliance Plight”, 2023-07-25">[10]</a>. Under the bill, the freeze “extends indefinitely beyond the point when the parties reach a good faith impasse to the date the parties reach agreement.” <a href="https://www.steptoe-johnson.com/news/house-passes-two-major-changes-to-collective-bargaining-law/" title="Robert M. Steptoe Jr. | Steptoe &amp; Johnson PLLC, “House Passes Two Major Changes to Collective Bargaining Law”, 2026-07-13">[11]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> In later contracts there is no arbitration exit. The freeze “applies not just to first contracts, but rather to all employers and all contracts” <a href="https://www.steptoe-johnson.com/news/house-passes-two-major-changes-to-collective-bargaining-law/" title="Robert M. Steptoe Jr. | Steptoe &amp; Johnson PLLC, “House Passes Two Major Changes to Collective Bargaining Law”, 2026-07-13">[11]</a>, while arbitration covers only talks “for the purpose of establishing an initial collective bargaining agreement” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill's added protection from cuts starts only after a deadlock, and it can hold pay flat with no end date. A frozen paycheck shrinks as living costs rise, which hurts the low-paid workers Morgan cares about most.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 5:</strong> By helping workers reach union contracts, it spreads pay gains that reduce inequality.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Canadian evidence supports the link between this process and more first contracts. A Center for American Progress review reports: “In Ontario, another researcher estimated that the proportion of negotiations that successfully reached a first contract increased up to 14 percentage points with automatic first-contract arbitration versus nonautomatic arbitration.” <a href="https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-faster-labor-contracts-act-should-be-just-the-starting-point-for-labor-law-reform/" title="Center for American Progress, “The Faster Labor Contracts Act Should Be Just the Starting Point for Labor Law Reform - Center for American Progress”, July 7, 2026">[12]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Union contracts are linked to higher pay. EPI reports: “A worker covered by a union contract earns 12.8% more in wages than a peer in a nonunionized workplace, on average.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/press/new-report-details-the-benefits-of-unions-to-workers-communities-and-democracy/" title="Economic Policy Institute (Press Releases), “New report details the benefits of unions to workers, communities, and democracy”, 2025-08-20">[13]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The gains help close racial pay gaps: “Black and Hispanic workers get a larger boost from unionization than their white counterparts.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Women also gain: “The wages of women represented by a union are 9.8% higher than those of nonunionized women.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/press/new-report-details-the-benefits-of-unions-to-workers-communities-and-democracy/" title="Economic Policy Institute (Press Releases), “New report details the benefits of unions to workers, communities, and democracy”, 2025-08-20">[13]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> Removing a barrier to union contracts extends access to higher pay and smaller racial pay gaps. That advances Morgan's concern for equal opportunity and a fairer share for people facing unequal treatment.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The pay figures compare union and non-union workers in general, not contracts set by this panel.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The Canadian study counts whether talks “successfully reached a first contract” <a href="https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-faster-labor-contracts-act-should-be-just-the-starting-point-for-labor-law-reform/" title="Center for American Progress, “The Faster Labor Contracts Act Should Be Just the Starting Point for Labor Law Reform - Center for American Progress”, July 7, 2026">[12]</a>. EPI compares a union worker with “a peer in a nonunionized workplace, on average.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/press/new-report-details-the-benefits-of-unions-to-workers-communities-and-democracy/" title="Economic Policy Institute (Press Releases), “New report details the benefits of unions to workers, communities, and democracy”, 2025-08-20">[13]</a> Neither measures pay in contracts a panel imposes.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The panel must weigh “the wages and benefits other employers in the same business provide their employees.” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a> Most of those employers are non-union: union membership was “only 5.9 percent of private-sector workers in 2024.” <a href="https://www.heritage.org/government-regulation/report/labor-law-reform-part-1-diagnosing-the-issues-exploring-current" title="Heritage Foundation, “Labor Law Reform Part 1: Diagnosing the Issues, Exploring Current Proposals | The Heritage Foundation”, October 9, 2025">[6]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's own Canada source warns that its estimate “may be optimistic given Canada’s union density of 30 percent, compared with just 10 percent overall in the United States” <a href="https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-faster-labor-contracts-act-should-be-just-the-starting-point-for-labor-law-reform/" title="Center for American Progress, “The Faster Labor Contracts Act Should Be Just the Starting Point for Labor Law Reform - Center for American Progress”, July 7, 2026">[12]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> A panel can tie pay to non-union rates and award no union pay gain. The PRO's evidence does not show that contracts set under this bill raise pay or narrow racial or gender pay gaps.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 6:</strong> It opens a path to protection from arbitrary firing and a way to challenge unfair treatment.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Private employment in most states is generally at will, meaning a boss can fire someone without giving a good reason: “employers can fire workers for almost any reason, without notice, and with no severance pay requirement” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A 2020 nationwide survey found unequal exposure to reported unfair firing: “Fifty percent of all Black workers and 52% of all Hispanic workers reported experiences with unfair dismissal, compared with 45% of white workers.” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Union contracts usually require a reason for discipline or firing: “These contracts typically have provisions that require employers to have a proper, documented, performance-related reason for disciplining or dismissing a worker” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Contracts also usually provide a process to “allow workers and the union to challenge unfair discipline or terminations” <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Helping workers secure first contracts expands access to these protections and a fair hearing. That serves Morgan's concern for people exposed to arbitrary or unequal treatment.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill requires no protection against unfair firing.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The panel's decision “shall be based on” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a> five factors: the employer's finances, its size and type, living costs, workers' ability to support their families, and pay at similar firms. None covers firing, discipline, or a way to challenge them.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> EPI describes what bargained contracts “typically” include <a href="https://www.epi.org/publication/unions-promote-racial-equity/" title="Josh Bivens, Celine McNicholas, Kyle K. Moore, and Margaret Poydock | Economic Policy Institute, “Unions promote racial equity”, 2023-07-31">[14]</a>, not terms this bill requires.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> A panel can impose a two-year contract with no fair-firing rule and no way to challenge unfair discipline. The PRO's firing data show who is vulnerable, not that this bill protects them.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 7:</strong> It offers a way to settle first-contract disputes with fewer strikes and lockouts.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> After mediation fails, the bill requires a binding settlement process: “the Service shall refer the dispute to a 3-person arbitration panel” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5408/BILLS-119hr5408eh.htm" title="H.R.5408, bill text, June 9, 2026">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Canadian research supports this benefit: “One study found that these laws reduced work stoppages on first-contract negotiations in the private sector by at least 50 percent.” <a href="https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-faster-labor-contracts-act-should-be-just-the-starting-point-for-labor-law-reform/" title="Center for American Progress, “The Faster Labor Contracts Act Should Be Just the Starting Point for Labor Law Reform - Center for American Progress”, July 7, 2026">[12]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Such disruptions can hurt workers. The Century Foundation describes “strikes, lockouts, and other labor disruptions that can be devastating for workers” <a href="https://tcf.org/content/commentary/trumps-war-on-the-labor-peacemakers/" title="The Century Foundation, analysis, March 31, 2025">[15]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> A route to a contract with less disruption protects workers from hardship while they seek fair terms, giving Morgan another reason to favor the bill.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill does not ban lockouts, and it weakens strikes, workers' main source of bargaining power.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill's text has no ban on strikes or lockouts (an employer shutting workers out to pressure them). Lawyers at Proskauer note that it “raises questions about the lawfulness of strikes and lockouts during these first contract negotiations.” <a href="https://www.laborrelationsupdate.com/2026/06/faster-is-not-always-better-house-passes-bill-seeking-radical-change-in-first-contract-bargaining/" title="Michael Lebowich, Joshua Fox, Daniel H.
