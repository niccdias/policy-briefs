title: "S.1582 × Security – Societal"
---

**Bill:** [S.1582](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1582), the GENIUS Act. Judged as of July 17, 2025, when Congress last voted on it.

**Value:** Security – Societal. A person who holds this value finds these things very important:

- that their country is secure and stable
- to have a strong state that can defend its citizens
- that their country protect itself against all threats

*Read "their country" as the United States.*

**Survey question:** “Imagine a person named Morgan. It's very important to Morgan [statement]. Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor or oppose S.1582?” Each respondent saw one of the statements above.

## Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor S.1582?

<table class="pc">
<tr><th>PRO</th><th>CON</th></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Claim:</strong> Morgan's value of a secure United States with a strong state gives Morgan a reason to favor S.1582 because it strengthens the state's tools against terrorist funding, drug cartels and efforts to evade restrictions on hostile states.</p></td><td><p><strong>Claim:</strong> The PRO counts new duties on U.S. stablecoin issuers as added protection, but existing law already covered most of them, and the illegal money it cites moves through foreign coins, foreign dealers and private wallets that the bill leaves open.</p></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Main effect:</strong> The bill would require approved issuers of payment stablecoins—digital money designed to hold a fixed cash value—to be able to block illegal payments. An approved U.S. issuer “may issue payment stablecoins only if the issuer has the technological capability to comply, and will comply, with the terms of any lawful order” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. These orders can require an issuer “to seize, freeze, burn, or prevent the transfer of payment stablecoins issued by the person” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</p></td><td><p><strong>Main effect:</strong> Not disputed.</p></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 1:</strong> The bill would strengthen the state's ability to detect and disrupt cartel payments.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> This targets a major route for crime money. Chainalysis's January 2025 report on 2024 found “stablecoins now occupying the majority of all illicit transaction volume (63% of all illicit transactions)” <a href="https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2025-crypto-crime-report-introduction/" title="Chainalysis Team, Chainalysis, “2025 Crypto Crime Trends: Illicit Volumes Portend Record Year as On-Chain Crime Becomes Increasingly Diverse and Professionalized”, 2025-01-15">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> FinCEN, Treasury's financial crime agency, reports that Mexican crime groups buy fentanyl ingredients and equipment from Chinese suppliers “in virtual currency, including bitcoin, ether, monero, and tether, among others” <a href="https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/advisory/2024-06-20/FinCEN-Supplemental-Advisory-on-Fentanyl-508C.pdf" title="Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Supplemental Advisory on the Procurement of Precursor Chemicals and Manufacturing Equipment Used for the Synthesis of Illicit Fentanyl and Other Synthetic Opioids (FIN-2024-A002)”, 2024-06-20">[3]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill requires approved U.S. issuers to identify customers and check their identities: “identification and verification of account holders with the permitted payment stablecoin issuer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. It also requires “monitoring and reporting of any suspicious transaction relevant to a possible violation of law or regulation” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> These reports give investigators useful leads. FinCEN says reporting leads to “the identification of suspected criminal and terrorist activity and the initiation of investigations” <a href="https://fincen.gov/news/speeches/prepared-remarks-fincen-director-kenneth-blanco-chainalysis-blockchain-symposium" title="Kenneth A. Blanco, Director, Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Prepared Remarks of FinCEN Director Kenneth A. Blanco at Chainalysis Blockchain Symposium”, 2019-11-15">[4]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Each approved issuer must certify its safeguards within 180 days of approval and annually after that. These programs target money laundering—hiding where crime money comes from—and must be “reasonably designed to prevent the permitted payment stablecoin issuer from facilitating money laundering, in particular, facilitating money laundering for cartels and organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Knowingly false certifications bring “the criminal penalties set forth under section 1001 of title 18, United States Code” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 4(f) bars anyone with a felony conviction involving money laundering or funding terrorism from serving as “an officer of a payment stablecoin issuer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> or “a director of a payment stablecoin issuer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill also requires rules for monitoring attempts to hide transfers. Section 9(d)(3) calls for standards covering “digital asset mixing services, tumblers, or other similar services that mix payment stablecoins in such a way as to make such transaction or the identity of the transaction parties less identifiable” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Identity checks, reports and safeguards backed by penalties strengthen the state's ability to trace cartel money and disrupt the supply chains that bring fentanyl into the United States.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill's checks stop at an issuer's own customers, while cartel money moves through later holders, foreign dealers and other coins.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Stablecoin firms already fell under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), the main U.S. law against money laundering. FinCEN, Treasury's financial crime agency, said in 2019 that “accepting and transmitting activity denominated in stablecoins makes you a money transmitter under the BSA” <a href="https://fincen.gov/news/speeches/prepared-remarks-fincen-director-kenneth-blanco-chainalysis-blockchain-symposium" title="Kenneth A. Blanco, Director, Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Prepared Remarks of FinCEN Director Kenneth A. Blanco at Chainalysis Blockchain Symposium”, 2019-11-15">[4]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Reporting suspicious transfers was already required. FinCEN's 2024 fentanyl advisory restates the existing rule: “A financial institution is required to file a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) if it knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect a transaction conducted or attempted by, at, or through the financial institution involves funds derived from illegal activity” <a href="https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/advisory/2024-06-20/FinCEN-Supplemental-Advisory-on-Fentanyl-508C.pdf" title="Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Supplemental Advisory on the Procurement of Precursor Chemicals and Manufacturing Equipment Used for the Synthesis of Illicit Fentanyl and Other Synthetic Opioids (FIN-2024-A002)”, 2024-06-20">[3]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill's new checks cover “identification and verification of account holders with the permitted payment stablecoin issuer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Brookings explains the limit: “while an issuer or exchange can vet the customer with whom it interacts directly, it has little control over where stablecoins go when they are used to execute payment transactions by the holder” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> That gap is where most of the crime happens. The Financial Action Task Force (FATF), the global standard setter against money laundering, found: “The majority of illicit activity in stablecoins occurs in the secondary market” <a href="https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/publications/targeted-report-on-stablecoins-and-unhosted-wallets.pdf" title="Financial Action Task Force (FATF), “Targeted Report on Stablecoins and Unhosted Wallets – Peer-to-Peer Transactions”, 2026-03 (March 2026)">[6]</a>. The secondary market is trading between holders after the issuer sells the coin.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Cartels cash out abroad through unlicensed crypto dealers in countries with weak rules. FATF says drug money in stablecoins is “often exchanged for fiat currency via unlicensed or unregistered VASPs, including OTC brokers, in jurisdictions with weak or non-existent AML/CFT controls” <a href="https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/publications/targeted-report-on-stablecoins-and-unhosted-wallets.pdf" title="Financial Action Task Force (FATF), “Targeted Report on Stablecoins and Unhosted Wallets – Peer-to-Peer Transactions”, 2026-03 (March 2026)">[6]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's 63% figure does not measure cartel money. Chainalysis says that, in general, “our totals exclude revenue from non-crypto-native crime, such as traditional drug trafficking” <a href="https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2025-crypto-crime-report-introduction/" title="Chainalysis Team, Chainalysis, “2025 Crypto Crime Trends: Illicit Volumes Portend Record Year as On-Chain Crime Becomes Increasingly Diverse and Professionalized”, 2025-01-15">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Cartels can switch coins. FinCEN lists their payments in “bitcoin, ether, monero, and tether” <a href="https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/advisory/2024-06-20/FinCEN-Supplemental-Advisory-on-Fentanyl-508C.pdf" title="Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Supplemental Advisory on the Procurement of Precursor Chemicals and Manufacturing Equipment Used for the Synthesis of Illicit Fentanyl and Other Synthetic Opioids (FIN-2024-A002)”, 2024-06-20">[3]</a>, and only tether is a stablecoin. FinCEN adds that cartels and their suppliers “have demonstrated an ability to adapt rapidly to changes in the regulatory or law enforcement environment” <a href="https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/advisory/2024-06-20/FinCEN-Supplemental-Advisory-on-Fentanyl-508C.pdf" title="Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Supplemental Advisory on the Procurement of Precursor Chemicals and Manufacturing Equipment Used for the Synthesis of Illicit Fentanyl and Other Synthetic Opioids (FIN-2024-A002)”, 2024-06-20">[3]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The rule on mixing services, which hide who sent coins, only orders future standards, due “Not later than 3 years after the date of enactment of this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Certifications, bans on convicted officers and future standards police U.S. issuers, not cartels. The routes cartels use stay as they were.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 2:</strong> The bill would require approved issuers to be able to freeze terrorist funds.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Taking stablecoins away from terrorist networks has a practical use. In March 2025, the Justice Department reported a court-authorized seizure of “Approximately $200,000 in USDT Intended to Support the Terrorist Activities of Harakat al-Muqawama al-Islamiyya (Hamas)” <a href="https://www.justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/justice-department-disrupts-hamas-terrorist-financing-scheme-through-seizure" title="U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. Attorney&#x27;s Office, District of Columbia, “Justice Department Disrupts Hamas Terrorist Financing Scheme Through Seizure of Cryptocurrency”, 2025-03-27">[7]</a>. USDT is Tether's dollar stablecoin.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Issuers can block access to their coins. Chainalysis reports that “Tether has frozen addresses of concern linked to scams, terrorist financing” <a href="https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2025-crypto-crime-report-introduction/" title="Chainalysis Team, Chainalysis, “2025 Crypto Crime Trends: Illicit Volumes Portend Record Year as On-Chain Crime Becomes Increasingly Diverse and Professionalized”, 2025-01-15">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 4(a)(5)(A)(iv) requires approved U.S. issuers to maintain “technical capabilities, policies, and procedures to block, freeze, and reject specific or impermissible transactions that violate Federal or State laws, rules, or regulations” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill would build the means to disable terrorist funds into approved stablecoins. Depriving terrorist networks of usable money strengthens the state's ability to protect Americans.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> Freezing terrorist funds already worked before the bill; the hard part is finding the money, and the bill does not help with that.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's example happened under current law. The March 2025 case was “the seizure of approximately $201,400 (based on current value) in cryptocurrency” <a href="https://www.justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/justice-department-disrupts-hamas-terrorist-financing-scheme-through-seizure" title="U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. Attorney&#x27;s Office, District of Columbia, “Justice Department Disrupts Hamas Terrorist Financing Scheme Through Seizure of Cryptocurrency”, 2025-03-27">[7]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Issuers already freeze funds on their own: “stablecoin issuers often freeze funds if they are made aware of their use by illicit actors” <a href="https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2025-crypto-crime-report-introduction/" title="Chainalysis Team, Chainalysis, “2025 Crypto Crime Trends: Illicit Volumes Portend Record Year as On-Chain Crime Becomes Increasingly Diverse and Professionalized”, 2025-01-15">[2]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A freeze order must name its target. It must specify “the payment stablecoins or accounts subject to blocking with reasonable particularity” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Yet “a stablecoin issuer cannot easily know or control who will ultimately be using its stablecoins after issuance” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Freezes come late. Brookings finds that “current practices to block and freeze accounts may be too slow and too late to prevent illicit transactions” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Terrorist financiers already work around these controls. FATF reports that they combine stablecoins with private wallets and hiding tools “to circumvent compliance controls” <a href="https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/publications/targeted-report-on-stablecoins-and-unhosted-wallets.pdf" title="Financial Action Task Force (FATF), “Targeted Report on Stablecoins and Unhosted Wallets – Peer-to-Peer Transactions”, 2026-03 (March 2026)">[6]</a>. Their campaigns reuse codes and addresses, “allowing financing operations to persist despite enforcement takedowns” <a href="https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/publications/targeted-report-on-stablecoins-and-unhosted-wallets.pdf" title="Financial Action Task Force (FATF), “Targeted Report on Stablecoins and Unhosted Wallets – Peer-to-Peer Transactions”, 2026-03 (March 2026)">[6]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Making a freeze button mandatory adds little when issuers already have one. It does not find hidden funds or stop transfers through private wallets.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 3:</strong> The bill would strengthen sanctions against hostile states by targeting a route used to evade them.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Sanctions are restrictions on dealings with listed people, firms or governments. Treasury found that Russian elites used dollar stablecoins to get around them: “Russian elites sought to exploit digital assets—in particular U.S. dollar-backed stablecoins—to evade U.S. and international sanctions, further enriching themselves and the Kremlin” <a href="https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2735" title="U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Treasury Exposes Money Laundering Network Using Digital Assets to Evade Sanctions”, 2024-12-04">[8]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill requires approved U.S. issuers to maintain “an effective economic sanctions compliance program, including verification of sanctions lists, consistent with Federal law” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> It also directs Treasury to coordinate with issuers, where possible, before blocking a foreign person's property: “shall, to the best of the Secretary's ability, coordinate with a permitted payment stablecoin issuer before taking any action to block and prohibit transactions in property and interests in property of a foreign person” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Required sanctions checks and preparation to block listed funds strengthen U.S. pressure on hostile states and the networks that move their money. That supports a state able to defend the country against foreign threats.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The sanctions duties repeat powers Treasury already had, and the evasion the PRO cites ran through foreign firms and coins that the new duties do not reach.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> U.S. firms already must freeze sanctioned funds. After Treasury's December 2024 action, “all property and interests in property of the blocked persons described above that are in the United States or in the possession or control of U.S. persons are blocked and must be reported to OFAC” <a href="https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2735" title="U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Treasury Exposes Money Laundering Network Using Digital Assets to Evade Sanctions”, 2024-12-04">[8]</a>. OFAC is Treasury's sanctions office.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill keeps that power as it was: “Nothing in this Act shall alter the existing authority of the Secretary of the Treasury to block, restrict, or limit transactions involving payment stablecoins” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The advance coordination is optional. Treasury acts only “to the best of the Secretary's ability” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> and “is not required to notify any permitted payment stablecoin issuer of any intended action” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The PRO's Russian network moved money through a Russian exchange and Tether's coin, USDT. A network company “leveraged OFAC-sanctioned Russian cryptocurrency exchange, Garantex OU, to process USDT transactions after its date of designation” <a href="https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2735" title="U.S. Department of the Treasury, “Treasury Exposes Money Laundering Network Using Digital Assets to Evade Sanctions”, 2024-12-04">[8]</a>. The bill's sanctions-program duty binds only approved issuers, each “a person formed in the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Freezes push hostile states toward coins that cannot be frozen. FATF says freezing Tether coins at addresses tied to Iran's Revolutionary Guard “may encourage sanctioned Iranian entities to move towards other stablecoins, such as DAI, that do not have a freeze function” <a href="https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/publications/targeted-report-on-stablecoins-and-unhosted-wallets.pdf" title="Financial Action Task Force (FATF), “Targeted Report on Stablecoins and Unhosted Wallets – Peer-to-Peer Transactions”, 2026-03 (March 2026)">[6]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> CSIS, reviewing the law after it passed, found: “The act also maintains gaps that have the potential to weaken U.S. national security by allowing adversaries to influence U.S. financial infrastructure, expand gray-zone financial operations, evade sanctions, and launder stolen funds” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill gives Treasury no sanctions tool it lacked. The foreign exchanges and unfreezable coins that hostile states use stay outside its reach.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 4:</strong> The bill would give the United States more power over foreign issuers serving its market.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Offshore issuance matters: Tether had blocked direct purchases and redemptions by U.S. citizens, yet “USDT continues to be one of the most traded coins” <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-05-23/stablecoin-legislation-must-tackle-tether-directly" title="bloomberg.com, “Stablecoin Legislation Must Tackle Tether Directly - Bloomberg”, May 23, 2025">[10]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 8(a)(1) bars foreign coins from being offered through crypto service firms in the United States “unless the foreign payment stablecoin issuer has the technological capability to comply and complies with the terms of any lawful order” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> After notice and continued refusal to comply, Treasury can prohibit crypto service firms from “facilitating secondary trading of payment stablecoins issued by the foreign payment stablecoin issuer in the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Firms that knowingly break that ban face “not more than $100,000 per violation per day” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A foreign issuer that knowingly keeps publicly offering coins after a noncompliance finding faces “not more than $1,000,000 per violation per day” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Treasury may also ask a court “to bar the foreign payment stablecoin issuer from engaging in financial transactions in the United States or with United States persons” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill's broader restriction on coins sold through crypto service firms starts three years after enactment: “beginning on the date that is 3 years after the date of enactment of this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. The foreign-issuer route requires home-country rules “comparable to the regulatory and supervisory regime established under this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> and being “registered with the Comptroller” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>, a federal bank regulator.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> That route also requires the issuer's home country to be “not subject to comprehensive economic sanctions by the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Registered foreign issuers must “consent to United States jurisdiction relating to the enforcement of this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> and face continuing “reporting, supervision, and examination requirements as determined by the Comptroller” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Treasury can revoke registration when an issuer “presents economic sanctions evasion, money laundering, or other illicit finance risks” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Access to U.S. crypto firms gives the state a way to demand compliance from offshore issuers. That puts at risk the public resources a strong state needs to defend its people.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill gives holders claims on private assets that weaken the case for a taxpayer rescue.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> The cited 2023 rescue used existing bank-failure law: “federal regulators invoked the systemic risk exception in the Federal Deposit Insurance Act and bailed out Circle and SVB’s other uninsured depositors” <a href="https://www.ineteconomics.org/perspectives/blog/time-bomb-how-uninsured-stablecoins-and-crypto-derivatives-threaten-financial-and-economic-stability" title="ineteconomics.org, analysis, October 6, 2025">[14]</a>. That was protection for a failed bank’s depositors before S.1582.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 11 puts coin holders first in claims on required reserves. CRS explains: “The bill would grant stablecoin holders priority over all other claims against the issuer in bankruptcy” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12553/IN12553.5.pdf" title="Congressional Research Service, report IN12553, July 18, 2025">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> If required reserves are missing, holders’ claims on the failed issuer’s other assets “shall have first priority over any other claim” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>, up to the missing amount.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> If the issuer asks and the court finds reserves available to share among holders, the court must use “best efforts to enter a final order to begin distributions under this paragraph not later than 14 days after the date of the required hearing” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> These rules protect holders’ recovery from private assets and seek earlier payouts. The PRO’s earlier bank rescue does not establish that this added protection creates a new need to spend the public resources used to defend citizens.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 4:</strong> The bill would put a federal seal on the payment tool that terrorists and sanctioned states already favor, while much of its use stays out of U.S. view.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Sanctions are a key U.S. defense: “the United States can pressure bad actors from Iran to Russia to China by cutting off access to our financial system and the dollar using sanctions” <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2025-07-17/html/CREC-2025-07-17-pt1-PgH3427.htm" title="Waters, floor speech, Congressional Record, July 17, 2025">[20]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Within crypto, stablecoins are already the favored way around them. In 2023 they were used in “80% of all cryptocurrency transactions conducted by sanctioned regimes and terrorist groups” <a href="https://www.ineteconomics.org/perspectives/blog/time-bomb-how-uninsured-stablecoins-and-crypto-derivatives-threaten-financial-and-economic-stability" title="ineteconomics.org, analysis, October 6, 2025">[14]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> That use is rising: “Adversarial use of stablecoins to evade sanctions has increased substantially in the last year.” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The bill's anti-money-laundering duties fall on issuers, but an issuer “has little control over where stablecoins go when they are used to execute payment transactions by the holder” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> “Under GENIUS, the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) does not regulate digital-asset intermediaries or decentralized protocols through which digital assets like stablecoins circulate. This creates a gap that malign actors exploit.” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a> The Bank Secrecy Act is the main U.S. law against money laundering. The passage means that law does not reach many of the middlemen and software through which coins move.</li><li><strong>Response:</strong> The bill's power to freeze coins does not close the gap: “current practices to block and freeze accounts may be too slow and too late to prevent illicit transactions” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>. And the bill gives regulators years to write rules for spotting crime: “Not later than 3 years after the date of enactment of this Act, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network shall issue public guidance and notice and comment rulemaking” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Growing this tool under a federal seal gives terrorists and hostile states more dollar channels that U.S. agents cannot watch or block in time. That blunts sanctions, one of the main ways the country protects itself.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill strengthens sanctions enforcement against an existing payment tool.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Its issuer duties include “monitoring and reporting of any suspicious transaction relevant to a possible violation of law or regulation” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> and “technical capabilities, policies, and procedures to block, freeze, and reject specific or impermissible transactions that violate Federal or State laws, rules, or regulations” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A permitted issuer may issue coins “only if the issuer has the technological capability to comply, and will comply, with the terms of any lawful order” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Such orders can require issuers to “prevent the transfer of payment stablecoins issued by the person” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Freezing has already disrupted sanctions evasion. Describing the March 2025 Garantex crackdown, Elliptic reports that “Tether subsequently froze approximately $28 million in USDT belonging to Garantex customers” <a href="https://www.elliptic.co/insights/ruble-backed-stablecoins-the-importance-of-identifying-indirect-sanctions-exposure/" title="Dr. Kelly Coulter / Elliptic, “Ruble-backed stablecoins: the importance of identifying indirect sanctions exposure”, 2025-08-28">[21]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Existing duties also reach middlemen that qualify as money transmitters: “both centralized and decentralized platforms engaged in money transmission must register with FinCEN to comply with the BSA/AML laws” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48883/R48883.6.pdf" title="Congressional Research Service, report R48883, April 17, 2026">[22]</a>. FinCEN is Treasury’s financial-crime agency; these are the federal anti-money-laundering rules. Section 4 says “Nothing in this Act shall restrict the authority of the Secretary of the Treasury” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> to enforce that law.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 9’s three-year deadline covers further detection, reporting and risk-management standards, including “Standards for payment stablecoin issuers' systems and practices to monitor transactions on blockchains” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Section 13 separately gives regulators one year to “promulgate regulations to carry out this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. The deadline for further standards does not suspend the core duties in section 4.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The PRO’s figures describe misuse that already existed. Delayed detection does not erase the added duty to monitor, report and freeze funds. Those powers strengthen the state’s ability to disrupt hostile financing.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 5:</strong> The bill lets companies issue dollar coins without a check for control by hostile foreign powers.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Regulators may refuse a license only on safety grounds. A regulator “shall only deny a substantially complete application received under subsection (a) if the regulator determines that the activities of the applicant would be unsafe or unsound based on the factors described in subsection (c)” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Those factors cover money, management and cash-in plans. None asks whether a foreign power controls the applicant.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> If regulators do not decide within 120 days, “the application shall be deemed approved” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Unlike traditional financial firms, “issuers are not subject to an intelligence- or Department of the Treasury–led assessment of foreign influence or security risks, meaning state-licensed issuers could be backed by entities linked to adversarial governments or sanctioned actors” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/stabilizing-us-financial-leadership-why-congress-must-get-stablecoin-regulation-right" title="Center for Strategic and International Studies, “Stabilizing U.S. Financial Leadership: Why Congress Must Get Stablecoin Regulation Right”, April 30, 2025">[11]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Foreign issuers get a way in too. Their U.S. registration “shall be deemed approved on the date that is 30 days after the date the Comptroller receives the registration” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>, unless it is rejected. The Comptroller is a federal bank regulator.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> That path “does not require rigorous screening of foreign governance structures” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a>. “Insufficient oversight of ultimate ownership and controlling interests might allow adversaries to influence an FPSI, creating national security risks.” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a> An FPSI is a foreign stablecoin issuer.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The biggest issuer is foreign and has been tied to crime. Tether is “the industry’s dominant issuer and biggest risk” <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-05-23/stablecoin-legislation-must-tackle-tether-directly" title="bloomberg.com, “Stablecoin Legislation Must Tackle Tether Directly - Bloomberg”, May 23, 2025">[10]</a>. Its “USDT coins have been seized and forfeited in cases involving fraud, terrorist financing, sanctions violations and other criminal activity” <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-05-23/stablecoin-legislation-must-tackle-tether-directly" title="bloomberg.com, “Stablecoin Legislation Must Tackle Tether Directly - Bloomberg”, May 23, 2025">[10]</a>.</li><li><strong>Response:</strong> Treasury must first find a foreign country's rules “comparable to the regulatory and supervisory regime established under this Act” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. But “if the Treasury defines comparability rules loosely, issuers will flock to the most permissive jurisdiction and still access the U.S. market” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Firms tied to hostile governments could run part of the country's dollar payments with a U.S. license. That gives foes a foothold inside the U.S. payment system.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill checks owners and gives officials grounds to exclude risky foreign issuers.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Federal licensing reviews the “competence, experience, and integrity of the officers, directors, and principal shareholders of the applicant, its subsidiaries, and parent company” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>, including their record of obeying the law. Ownership and parent companies are part of the review.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The 120-day clock starts only when an application “contains sufficient information for the primary Federal payment stablecoin regulator to render a decision on whether the applicant satisfies the factors” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. The clock is not a substitute for supplying information needed for those checks.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Foreign registration has a separate security screen. The federal bank regulator must consider “whether the foreign payment stablecoin issuer presents illicit finance risks to the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Treasury can revoke registration when “reasonable grounds exist for concluding that the foreign payment stablecoin issuer presents economic sanctions evasion, money laundering, or other illicit finance risks” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The foreign route excludes issuers based in countries “subject to comprehensive economic sanctions by the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Treasury can approve a country’s rules as comparable “only upon a recommendation from each other member of the Stablecoin Certification Review Committee” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>—the heads of the Federal Reserve and the FDIC, the bank-deposit insurer.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> A foreign issuer that refuses a lawful order faces a process leading to a ban on U.S. platform trading. Section 8 provides for “prohibiting digital asset service providers from facilitating secondary trading of payment stablecoins issued by the foreign payment stablecoin issuer in the United States” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>, subject to Treasury’s specified waiver powers. This reaches resale between users, not just sales by the issuer.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> The bill makes owners reviewable, gives foreign registration an illicit-finance test, and puts U.S. trading access at risk for refusing lawful orders. The absence of a separate intelligence review does not establish the unchecked foothold the PRO claims.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Point 6:</strong> The bill limits how far regulators can go to contain these risks.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> It caps capital rules at levels that “do not exceed requirements that are sufficient to ensure the ongoing operations of permitted payment stablecoin issuers” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. Capital is the owners' own money that absorbs losses first.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> It “exempts stablecoin issuers from the regulatory capital standards applied to traditional banks” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12553/IN12553.5.pdf" title="Congressional Research Service, report IN12553, July 18, 2025">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Licensing regulators “may not impose requirements in addition to the requirements specified under section 4” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Smaller issuers may choose a state regulator instead. “This allows a race to the bottom, where unscrupulous sponsors would seek the state with the most convenient rules.” <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2025-07-17/html/CREC-2025-07-17-pt1-PgH3405.htm" title="Public Citizen; Public Citizen., letter inserted in the Congressional Record, July 17, 2025">[23]</a></li><li><strong>Because:</strong> “These gaps hinder oversight of stablecoin flows and leave the Federal Reserve and the Department of the Treasury with limited leverage.” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/unstable-coins-stablecoin-regulation-market-structure-legislation-and-us-security-risks" title="csis.org, analysis, January 12, 2026">[9]</a></li><li><strong>So:</strong> As stablecoins grow, the bill holds back the officials who guard against financial and security threats. That leaves the state less able to defend its citizens from the dangers in Reasons 1–5.</li></ul></td><td><p><strong>Response:</strong> The bill’s limits leave officials substantial powers to control these risks.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> Bank capital rules are not the only way to protect payments. Brookings explains that, because issuers cannot take deposits and make loans as banks do, their safety rules “can be lower and simpler than for banks while still being sufficient to support the singleness of money” <a href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/next-steps-for-genius-payment-stablecoins/" title="brookings.edu, analysis, March 3, 2026">[5]</a>—keeping coins worth their promised dollar.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> Section 4 permits federal regulators to require added loss-absorbing funds: rules “may include capital buffers that are tailored to the business model and risk profile of permitted payment stablecoin issuers” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> For federally qualified issuers, the Comptroller, the federal bank chartering regulator, has authority “to issue such regulations and orders as necessary to ensure financial stability and implement subsection (a)” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. These are powers within section 4, which the licensing limit leaves intact.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> States must pass a federal check. Certification requires a unanimous finding that “the State-level regulatory regime meets or exceeds the standards and requirements described in subsection (a)” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a>. That is section 4’s baseline for backing, cash management and crime prevention.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> State issuers above $10 billion in issuance normally move to joint federal and state oversight, “unless granted a waiver by the federal regulator” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12553/IN12553.5.pdf" title="Congressional Research Service, report IN12553, July 18, 2025">[18]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> In emergencies, after at least 48 hours’ notice to the state regulator, the Federal Reserve can “take an enforcement action against a State qualified payment stablecoin issuer” <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1582/BILLS-119s1582es.htm" title="S.1582, bill text, June 17, 2025">[1]</a> for violations of the Act.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Choosing a state does not let an issuer discard the bill’s safety standards. Tailored capital rules, federal review and emergency enforcement give the state concrete tools to protect financial stability.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td></td><td><p><strong>New point:</strong> The PRO leaves out how regulated dollar payments support U.S. influence against rival financial systems.</p><ul><li><strong>Because:</strong> CSIS identifies the competing threat: projects such as mBridge seek “financial rails that operate beyond Western banking infrastructure” <a href="https://www.csis.org/analysis/stabilizing-us-financial-leadership-why-congress-must-get-stablecoin-regulation-right" title="Center for Strategic and International Studies, “Stabilizing U.S. Financial Leadership: Why Congress Must Get Stablecoin Regulation Right”, April 30, 2025">[11]</a>.</li><li><strong>Because:</strong> The Council on Foreign Relations links this bill to dollar growth: “The passage of the GENIUS Act is expected to lead to rapid growth in the circulation of bank-issued dollar stablecoins” <a href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/why-china-spooked-dollar-stablecoins-and-how-it-will-respond" title="cfr.org, analysis, August 21, 2025">[12]</a>. It explains the strategic effect: “In a world where dollar stablecoins circulate globally, Beijing risks losing not just monetary ground, but also political leverage” <a href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/why-china-spooked-dollar-stablecoins-and-how-it-will-respond" title="cfr.org, analysis, August 21, 2025">[12]</a>.</li><li><strong>So:</strong> Growth under the bill’s safeguards supports a dollar-based alternative to rival payment systems. That helps preserve the U.S. financial influence that Morgan’s security value makes important.</li></ul></td></tr>
<tr><td><p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> Because S.1582 would grow run-prone private dollars tied to U.S. banks and government debt, open new channels for terrorists and hostile states, and restrain the regulators who guard against both, Morgan's wish for a secure, stable and well-defended country gives Morgan a reason to oppose it.</p></td><td><p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> By overlooking stronger backing, recovery rights, sanctions controls and oversight, the PRO fails to show that S.1582 gives Morgan’s wish for a secure and stable United States a reason for opposition.</p></td></tr>
