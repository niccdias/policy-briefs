## Fixed quality standard

1. Reconstruct each PRO's bill change, empirical bridge and value link. Check material claims against source context. Distinguish fact, warranted inference and speculation. Supported small or secondary effects qualify; there is **no main/principal/substantial-effect eligibility threshold**. Magnitude belongs in later strength comparison.
2. A CON must identify a genuine defect in the actual PRO: a consequential unsupported necessary step, relevant contrary evidence, legal scope, relevant null results, documented changed practices or a demonstrated constraint. A missing necessary premise can fail without proof of the opposite. Do not require deductive certainty for ordinary causal predictions.
3. **Existing funding/activity, predating conditions, no earmark, no S.2-style bill-specific estimate or uncertain size do not alone rebut an ordinary inference from added eligible capacity or changed incentives to some effect.** Do not substitute a stronger claim, demand a guarantee, or present another possible outcome/independent opposing effect as a refutation. Baseline facts can remain useful notes even when the objection fails.
4. Test the whole actual PRO. If one independent supported branch carries its stated modest claim, an objection only to another branch does not defeat the claim. A partial challenge is allowed when a consequential step really is deficient; label exactly what remains unanswered. The word "partial" does not rescue a degree-only objection.
5. Do not impose a universal rule that a constraint must be independent of funded inputs and block every path. Ask whether this documented constraint undermines the particular inference actually claimed. Evidence of priorities/assignments may bear on how general funds are used, with dates and limits stated. Broad statutory eligibility alone does not prove a concrete task receives added support; assess an ordinary inference in context.
6. Review PRO just as critically: unsupported steps, scope overstatement, value drift, selective quotation, inappropriate extrapolation, comparisons with preferred alternatives, duplicates and padding. "Average American" does not mean non-Hispanic or immune from community spillovers. Preserve the exact value statements and the exact fixed Christian-traditionalist, average-American and USA profiles. Apply these boundaries: Tradition needs an identifiable inherited custom, religious practice or way of life, valued for its continuity with the past, and religious endorsement of a general moral goal alone does not establish that connection. For Universalism – Tolerance, general information or general compassion alone is insufficient. For Security – Societal, an isolated harm to individuals does not become societal by relabeling.
7. **Allow no CON.** Remove indefensible objections, never invent a replacement for symmetry. If the original already explicitly says no sound/supported rebuttal survives, classify it Remove (absence of rebuttal) and preserve useful facts as notes. This does not automatically establish PRO. Reassess paired CON after every PRO change. Genuine duplicate paths may be merged, with destination recorded; do not lose a distinct mechanism merely for similar beneficiaries. No required balance in counts.
8. Preserve sound reasoning, fix only the defective substantive step, and update claims/conclusions/shared objections to match surviving arguments. Then apply the ESL clarity requirements below to all surviving text, including arguments that need no substantive repair. Assess every New point and identify exactly which numbered PROs it applies to. Remove baseline-only shared objections; preserve their useful facts as comparison notes visible to coders.
9. Before delivering, conduct a consistency screen across **all retained CONs** for the same standard, particularly baseline-only, no-earmark, magnitude-only and stronger-claim objections. This is an internal consistency check of this one pass, not repeated reviews until you feel satisfied. Record clear unresolved empirical questions separately from logical defects.
10. Classify every problem you find, including problems in text you did not otherwise change:
    - **Confirmed defect:** the text misstates a source, overreaches its legal or factual scope, breaks a value boundary, misattributes a statement or gives coders an editorial verdict. Correct it or remove the point in this pass. Noting it is not enough.
    - **Genuine uncertainty:** the empirical or legal question is open after you read the source context. Log the precise question for Sol 6.1 Max. In the meantime, do not let the text claim more than the source shows.
    - **Human coding judgment:** reasonable reviewers could weigh a supported argument differently, and the page gives them what they need to decide. Leave it for them. Do not use this class to avoid fixing a defect.
    - **No defect:** the concern fails after you check the context. Record why.
11. Check a source's reach when you check its support. A statement about a whole bill or program does not establish a claim about one spending line or part. A legal clause must be read with its "subject to" clauses, exceptions and conditions. Keep qualifiers such as "up to the limit" and "formerly insured" when you paraphrase. Coder-visible notes and gap wording state facts and limits only. Editorial conclusions such as "this gap does not break the argument" belong in the ledger.

## ESL clarity within the same review

Write for an adult coder who reads English as a second language and has little or no prior knowledge of the bill, policy area, US government, legal system, relevant technical concepts or cited events. Clear language alone is not enough if understanding still depends on unstated background knowledge. Review every surviving argument and response for clarity, including those whose reasoning is sound. Rewrite wherever needed; already clear wording may stay. Apply the same standard to headings, shared objections, evidence notes and surrounding brief prose. Keep the supplied value statements, reference profiles and judgment-question wording exact.

- Use familiar, concrete words, short sentences and explicit actors. Keep one main claim or inference per sentence. Break up nested clauses, double negatives and long noun phrases. Avoid idioms, rhetorical flourishes and unexplained abbreviations.
- Name the person, agency, bill provision or outcome instead of using an ambiguous “it,” “this” or “they.” Define an essential legal or technical term briefly at first use. Use the same term for the same thing throughout the pair.
- Supply the background needed to understand the specific argument: who an agency or institution is, what it does, how the relevant rule or program currently works, and what the bill would change. Expanding an acronym alone may not explain its role. Explain why a cited fact or quotation matters. Use the supplied record; flag missing or substantively new background claims for evidence review instead of inventing them. Keep explanations brief and relevant.
- Make each point understandable with its response, notes and fixed bill/value context. Arguments are randomized, so do not rely on a definition or explanation appearing in an earlier argument. Briefly repeat essential context when it is otherwise unavailable on that page.
- Make the existing chain easy to follow: what the bill changes compared with law in force, how that change could affect the stated outcome, and why that outcome matters for the specified value. In a response, name the exact step challenged and explain the defect in plain English. Preserve the existing point structure and original numbers.
- Use direct headings that state the particular consequence. Avoid vague labels such as “It provides another route” or “It supplies material.” Do not add a value slogan to create a connection the reasoning does not support.
- Preserve every material condition, exception, attribution, uncertainty, population, direction and distinction between an additional effect and an existing activity. “Could” must not become “will”; a warning must not become an observed finding; “some” must not become “all.” Clarity is not permission to strengthen or weaken a claim, add a causal or value premise, remove a valid response, or hide an unresolved issue.
- Keep quotations verbatim and citations attached to the claims they support, with source IDs and namespaces unchanged. Explain necessary jargon in nearby plain prose. Read the source context before explaining what a quoted word such as “this” or “the package” refers to. If using a shorter exact excerpt or an unquoted paraphrase, check the source context and preserve its qualifications; never silently simplify words inside quotation marks. Retain source access and provenance.
- Use only the existing argument and evidence for wording changes. If a clear account requires a new premise or the value connection remains unclear, treat that as a substantive issue under the quality standard. Do not make a weak argument sound complete by supplying the missing bridge. Reassess the paired response after any substantive PRO change.

After rewriting a pair, compare the simpler text with the substantively reviewed claim. Check the actor, bill change, comparison, mechanism, value link, conditions, certainty and source meaning. Identify each item of background knowledge needed to follow the point, and confirm that it is explained on that page or in its fixed context. Confirm that the response still addresses the actual PRO and states any consequential branch it leaves unanswered. This is a fidelity check within the same pass, not a new open-ended review round. Do not use a reading-level score or a sentence-length target as proof of clarity or fidelity.

For example, “During an active investigation, the department may temporarily withhold these records” can become “While an investigation is active, the department can keep these records private for a limited time.” It must not become “The bill keeps witness identities private”: that supplies a different protection. This example illustrates wording only; establish the exact legal scope from the supplied bill before using either claim in a brief.
