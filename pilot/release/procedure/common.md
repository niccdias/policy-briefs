You are one step in a research procedure. For a U.S. bill and a personal value, models write four arguments: a PRO and a CON on whether the value gives a person a reason to favor the bill, and a PRO and a CON on whether it gives a reason to oppose it. A human panel later judges which connections are substantiated. You do not give a verdict on the bill or the value.

**Bill:** {bill}, {bill_title}. The bill's text is in the evidence base.

**When:** judge the bill as of {decision_date}, when Congress last voted on it: what it would do if it took effect then, compared with the law in force on that date. Later events, such as court rulings or new orders, do not change that question. Evidence published after that date, up to July 17, 2026, may still show what the bill would have done. If you use later evidence, explain why it bears on that fixed comparison.

**Value:** {value_name}. A person who holds this value finds these things very important:
{value_statements}
{reference_line}
Survey respondents answered this question, with one of the statements above: "Imagine a person named Morgan. It's very important to Morgan to [statement]. Does this value give Morgan a reason to favor or oppose {bill}?"

**Rules**

1. Give every path from the bill to the value that the evidence supports. Small or secondary effects count. There is no test that an effect must be a main, principal or substantial effect of the bill. Size is considered later, when a human compares the reasons. Evidence that the bill changes people's protection or risk counts even without figures on how many people are affected. State any limit that the evidence shows.
2. Keep the value inside its boundary.
   - Use the value statements and reference person above exactly as written.
   - **Tradition:** the argument must connect the bill to an identifiable inherited custom, religious practice or way of life, valued for its continuity with the past. Religious endorsement of a general moral goal alone is not enough.
   - **Universalism – Tolerance:** general information or general compassion alone is not enough.
   - **Security – Societal:** an isolated harm to individuals does not become a societal harm because it is described with a societal label.
3. Be accurate about strength and do not say which side is right overall. State a real limit or a real uncertainty when the evidence shows one. Each direction is judged on its own: a value can give Morgan a reason to favor a bill and also a reason to oppose it. So a CON must identify a genuine defect in the actual PRO point. Pointing to effects of the bill that cut the other way is not a defect, unless those effects show that the effect the PRO relies on would not happen. Do not invent an objection for balance. If a PRO point has no genuine defect, the CON writes exactly `N. No defensible CON retained.` for that point.
4. The bill's evidence base is in `evidence/`, listed in `evidence/INDEX.tsv`. Support factual claims with quotations, written exactly like this: “exact words from the source” [doc_id]. A quotation must match its source word for word, as one continuous passage, with no ellipses or added words inside the quotation marks.
5. You may search the web for more evidence when you need it to substantiate a claim. Every new source you cite must be dated on or before July 17, 2026. Save its full text as `output/sources/new_NN.txt` (new_01, new_02, ...), with the URL, title, author or publisher, date and date basis on the first lines. The date basis says how you know the date: printed publication or version date, search listing, or another stand-in date. Do not make a source with materially uncertain timing the only support for a claim. Cite each new source as [new_NN]. Log every search in `output/search_log.tsv` with three tab-separated columns: the claim it is meant to support, the query, and the sources you kept.
6. Write the argument as a structured outline, using the skeleton for your role given below. Make the logical relationships visible: label each premise **Because**, each inference **So**, and each response to a PRO point **Response**. Put one point on each line, so a reader can follow the argument at a glance. Each numbered point must be a separate path from the bill to the value; put the steps of one path under that point as Because and So lines rather than splitting them into several points.
7. Cut superfluous words. Use the fewest words that state each point fully. Leave out preamble, rhetorical language and repetition, and restate the bill or the value only where a point needs it. There is no length limit; length should come from substance, not wording.
8. Use the simplest words that are accurate. Write short, plain sentences for a reader with no background in law, policy or economics. Prefer everyday words: "tax on imports", not "ad valorem duty"; "ends", not "terminates". If a technical term cannot be avoided, explain it in plain words the first time. Quote only the words you need; your own words should be plainer than your sources.
9. Make each numbered point understandable alone. Arguments are shown in random order, so a reader may see this point first, with only its response, its notes and the bill and value context. Do not rely on an earlier point or another argument for a definition or explanation.
10. Name the person, agency, bill provision or outcome instead of writing an unclear "it", "this", "they", "the package" or "the policy". Do not leave out words that the reader must supply from an earlier sentence. Explain who an agency is and what it does when the point needs that.
11. Read the source context before you explain what a quoted word refers to. If a quotation is unclear alone, explain it outside the quotation marks using the source, or use a faithful paraphrase. Do not change words inside the quotation marks. If the source does not settle the referent, say that it does not. Do not use later evidence to supply the referent of an earlier statement or to set the historical baseline.
12. Describe the bill and its effects in plain, concrete words. Start with what changes, not with a statement that this is a bill or with a description of the method. Keep the bill's version, comparison date and evidence cutoff exact wherever you state them.
13. Check how far a source reaches before you rely on it. A statement about a whole bill or program does not support a claim about one part of it, unless the source says so. Read the whole legal provision, including any "subject to" clause, exception, condition and scope limit. Keep a source's qualifier when you paraphrase it, and attribute a statement only to the person or body that made it.
14. Do not write a verdict about your own text. Do not say that a gap does or does not break the argument, that a point stays or is kept, or that a standard is met. State the fact or the limit. If you find a defect in a claim, fix the claim or cut it. A note that mentions the defect does not fix it.
15. Use only the files in this folder and the web. Do not open any other file on this computer.
